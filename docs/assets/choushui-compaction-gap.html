The Choushui Compaction Gap

Land subsidence in central Taiwan is well monitored, heavily modelled, and still missing the one thing HydroPhysicsAI is built to supply: a differentiable rheology that carries memory.

01

Four findings that should change what you do next

Diagnosis

Your Sk model failed for a published reason — and the reason names the fix

Tsai & Hsu (2018) analysed multi-layer compaction and head data on this exact fan and concluded the deformation is visco-elasto-plastic: elastic, plastic and viscous components all present, with a time-delayed response. Lees et al. (2022), modelling 65 years of San Joaquin Valley subsidence, independently found residual clay compaction time constants are far larger than assumed — decades to centuries.

Your S = Sk · cumulative_drawdown is memory-less instantaneous plasticity: no elastic recovery, no viscous delay, no preconsolidation state. A single scalar cannot absorb a rheology. That is why per-site Sk fits at R² = 0.81 in-sample and collapses out-of-sample. The negative result in your README is correct and expected — not a data problem alone.

Collision

The forecasting track has direct prior art on the same fan

Patra & Chu (2023, J. Hydrol. Reg. Studies, 29 citations) is titled “Regional groundwater sequential forecasting using global and local LSTM models” — study area the Choushui River Alluvial Fan, daily fluctuations, multiple monitoring wells. That is the design of your GlobalForecastLSTM. Chang, Sun, Kow et al. (2025, J. Hydrol., 30 citations) run hybrid deep-learning level forecasting on “Taiwan’s largest alluvial fan”. Patra & Chu (2024) extend to ConvLSTM over the whole fan.

PHASE2_LITERATURE.md claim #3 — the global-vs-local positioning — cites none of these, and they are the closest work that exists. Fix the citations before submission.

Whitespace

The gap survives a much harder search

Across all of OpenAlex, "physics-informed" AND (subsidence OR land deformation OR InSAR) returns 52 works, and exactly one couples heads to subsidence with a PINN: Wang et al. (2025), Dezhou City, North China Plain — two citations. Nothing on Choushui. universal differential equation + hydrology returns two works, neither about subsidence.

hybrid physics machine learning land subsidence compaction returns zero. Six groups work this fan and several hold both halves — Ni and Shih-Jung Wang hold the poromechanics and the learning — but always in separate papers. Nobody has put a differentiable, memory-carrying compaction model here.

Threats

Two problems with the leveling plan that the literature states outright

Benchmark elevation change is not pure compaction — it carries tectonic vertical motion, and Taiwan’s vertical velocity field is published. And “learn Sk from hydrogeological attributes” has a documented uncertainty floor set by borehole density, established in a paper sited inside your study area. Both are addressable; neither is optional. See §04.

Ni Chuen-Fa is the one to watch

149 works, ~1,250 citations; 23 on subsidence or deformation, 18 on this fan. He publishes it as the Choushui River Fluvial Plain — much of it in TAO, IAHS proceedings and EGU abstracts, often as middle author — which is exactly how a citation-ranked search loses him.

He is the only person on the fan spanning both ends. Geodetic fusion: Lu & Ni 2016 cokrige precise leveling, GPS and PSInSAR across the plain for 1993–2008 — the fusion idea behind direction ③, already done geostatistically. Mechanics: Nugraha, Ni & Nguyen 2023, “from conceptual model to hydromechanical model in Choushui River Alluvial Fan”, plus real poromechanics with Ronaldo Borja (10.1016/j.jhydrol.2024.132108) and stochastic flow↔deformation coupling in Physics of Fluids. Learning: Liu, Ku & Ni 2025. Treat him as a likely reviewer — and, being in Taiwan, a plausible collaborator.

One finding of his to engage with directly: Nguyen & Ni (2024) report that half the major compaction on this fan occurs at shallow depth, where Lees et al. (2022) found over 90% of recent San Joaquin subsidence came from the lower confined aquifer. The depth partition here is genuinely unlike the canonical case, and it is contested — which turns your depth-resolved compaction-well data from a detail into an asset.

Shih-Jung Wang asks the question that bears hardest on your plan

Also NCU. His 2009–2025 programme is about how uncertainty in the geological model propagates into flow and subsidence predictions: first-order second-moment poroelasticity in heterogeneous media (2009), flow↔deformation coupling in randomly heterogeneous media (2013), a nonlinear stochastic poroelastic model for quantifying pumping and subsidence (2015), model uncertainty at Huwei Town in Yunlin — inside your study area (2022), and how borehole density controls which geostatistical properties are estimable at all (2025). That last one is a direct prior on whether your attribute network can work.

The critical observation still stands, and now it holds inside individual groups: the mechanics work and the machine-learning work do not overlap. Chu’s models are empirical spatial regressions of subsidence on drawdown. Ku’s are static susceptibility maps with no time dynamics. Tsai & Hsu have the rheology but fit it well-by-well, with no learning and no spatial generalization. Ni and Wang hold both halves — in separate papers. The bridge — a differentiable rheology whose parameters are learned as a function of hydrogeology across all sites — is unoccupied.

03

The physics you are missing, in the order it matters

  1. Effective stress. Compaction is driven by effective-stress increase as head falls. Framing references: Gambolati & Teatini 2015 (10.1002/2014wr016841); Guzy & Malinowska 2020 state-of-the-art review (10.3390/w12072051).
  2. Elastic vs inelastic, gated by preconsolidation head. Compaction is recoverable (small Ske) while head stays above the historical minimum, permanent (Skv, often 10–100× larger) below it. Your h₀ − running-min(h) is exactly a fixed preconsolidation assumption. Li et al. (2022) show the preconsolidation head is variable and separate the two storage modes (10.1016/j.jhydrol.2021.127420). Li & Wang (2025) estimate inelastic skeletal storativity from SAR plus head in the Beijing Plain (10.1016/j.ejrh.2024.102161) — the closest published analogue to what you are fitting.
  3. Delay. Thick aquitards drain slowly; the surface lags head by months to decades. This is the term your model has none of, and the one Tsai & Hsu found dominant here.
  4. Depth structure — contested on this fan. Tsai & Hsu found Young’s modulus rising with depth and distal→proximal. Lees et al. found over 90% of recent San Joaquin subsidence came from the lower confined aquifer. But Nguyen & Ni (2024) report half the major compaction here occurs at shallow depth. Your depth-resolved compaction-well data can address an open question, not just a detail.
  5. Other mechanisms exist. Clay dehydration was proposed for the Yunlin coastal area by Liu et al. (2001, 10.1007/s002540000193) — a non-poroelastic contribution your model would silently absorb into Sk.

So: Sk ~ exp(β₀ + β₁·distance_to_coast) regresses one parameter of a five-parameter rheology onto one covariate. The in-sample correlation of −0.68 is the marine-clay gradient showing through; out-of-sample failure is what happens when the omitted parameters — time constant, preconsolidation state, depth partition — vary independently across sites.

04

Two threats to the leveling plan

Threat 1 — leveling records tectonics too

Ching et al. (2011, JGR, 10.1029/2011jb008242) built Taiwan’s modern vertical velocity field from 1,843 precise-leveling benchmarks and 199 continuous GPS stations, 2000–2008: uplift of 0.2–18.5 mm/yr in the range interior, subsidence on the flanks and coastal plains, with western coastal rates departing from geologic rates because of groundwater pumping.

So benchmark elevation change is tectonic motion + anthropogenic compaction. On the coastal fan the pumping signal (~2 cm/yr, up to 6 cm/yr per Nguyen & Ni) swamps tectonics — but at proximal and inland benchmarks the compaction signal is small, so the ratio is worst exactly where your Sk fit is most fragile. Subtract a tectonic vertical-rate field, or fit a per-site linear offset, and report what fraction of variance it absorbs. Untreated, this alone can corrupt a per-site regression.

Threat 2 — the attribute covariates may not carry the information

Your plan learns rheological parameters from hydrogeological attributes — fine fraction, drainage path, depth, distance to coast. Shih-Jung Wang’s group has published precisely on that limit: geological-model uncertainty propagating into flow and subsidence simulations at Huwei, Yunlin (Tran, Wang & Nguyen 2022), uncertainty as a function of data sufficiency (10.1007/s10064-022-02832-7), and borehole density controlling which geostatistical properties are estimable at all (Tran, Wang & Dong 2025).

Read these before designing the attribute network. They set the expectation for how much of Sk is knowable from what you hold — and give you a citable reason if the answer turns out to be “not much”.

05

Observables you are not yet using

Cite the instrument
Hung, Hwang, Sneed (USGS), Chen & Chu 2021, WRR (10.1029/2020wr028194): magnetic rings at 25 depths to 300 m, 1 mm precision, tested across proximal, middle and distal fan. This is the reference for the compaction data you already hold, and nearly everyone on this fan cites it.
Bigger head network
Hung et al. 2024 (10.3389/feart.2024.1370626) work with 233 groundwater monitoring stations across four aquifers plus 50 continuous GNSS stations on this fan. You are modelling 61 wells.
Absolute gravity
K.-H. Chen, Hwang & Tanaka 2023 (10.1016/j.enggeo.2023.107021) estimate groundwater mass balance of Yunlin’s sandy aquifers from gravity — an independent constraint on storage change that is neither head nor deformation.
Fusion is taken
Hung et al. 2011, RSE (10.1016/j.rse.2010.11.007, 89 cites) already fuse PS-InSAR with leveling on this fan; Lu & Ni 2016 cokrige three sensor types. Pitch novelty as differentiability, not fusion.
06

Four directions, ranked

01

Differentiable visco-elasto-plastic compaction model, trained across all sites

Recommended

Replace the algebraic Sk with a small ODE carrying internal state — elastic strain, inelastic strain, relaxation time constant — and a neural network mapping site hydrogeology (fine-grained fraction, drainage-path length, depth-to-layer, distance-to-coast, layer thickness) to the rheological parameters. Train jointly against the 16 compaction wells (depth-resolved, high frequency) and the 556 leveling benchmarks (surface-only, annual) now retrievable from the wisenvr API. Score with the same leave-one-site-out gate.

Why this one: the exact intersection of your existing UDE machinery, the newly available leveling data, the published diagnosis of why simple models fail, and a worldwide literature gap of one paper. It also converts your current negative result into a motivation section.

02

Learned preconsolidation-head gate

A narrower version of ①: keep the algebraic form but make the elastic/inelastic switch a learned function of state rather than a running minimum. Cheaper, less novel, and a clean incremental result — best used as an ablation inside ①. Closest published analogue: Li & Wang 2025 on the Beijing Plain.

03

Joint head ↔ deformation inversion

Ali et al. (2021, 10.1016/j.envsoft.2021.105123) run it backwards, estimating heads from GPS deformation. A bidirectional model would let the 1,239 leveling points act as supervision on the head field — the same spatial-sparsity wall your PINN hit.

More occupied than it looks: Lu & Ni (2016) already cokrige leveling, GPS and PSInSAR here; Lu & Ni (2012) did “inversion of subsidence parameters” on this plain; Nguyen & Ni (2024) integrate InSAR with heads, multi-layer compaction and borehole logs. The differentiable version is open; the fusion framing is not.

04

Pure ML subsidence mapping

Avoid

Ku, Chu and Patra have covered ANN, PCA, spatial regression and ConvLSTM on this fan since 2020, and Liu, Ku & Ni 2025 (Sci. Rep.) add LSTM subsidence reconstruction with pumping-reduction scenarios driven by well electricity use. Entering here means competing on their turf with no mechanism of your own.

07

The next experiment

  1. Build the leveling panel: 556 sites × ~11 annual elevations, differenced to cumulative subsidence relative to each site’s first survey. Already cached as ls-wra-lsp-obs__choushui_panel.parquet.
  2. Remove the tectonic component — a per-site linear offset at minimum. Report how much variance it absorbs.
  3. Interpolate monthly heads to each leveling site with your existing IDW, then aggregate to the survey dates so head history and elevation change share a time base.
  4. Refit the current Sk regression on 556 sites instead of 14. This alone separates the two hypotheses.
  5. Fit the VEP-UDE with the parameter network under the same leave-one-site-out protocol, ablating: no-viscosity, no-elastic, fixed-preconsolidation, no-attribute-network.
  6. Report against Chu et al. 2021 as the published empirical baseline for this fan.

Step 4 is the fork in the road, and it costs an afternoon. If leave-one-site-out R² is still negative with 40× more sites, the wall was never sparsity — it is the model form, and direction ① is justified on evidence instead of assertion.

08

Caveats on the new data

Annual only
Leveling constrains long-term rheology and time constants, not seasonal dynamics. The compaction wells stay the only high-frequency signal — 16 sites on the fan.
GNSS starts 2020
The API’s GNSS record begins 2020-01-01, overlapping only the tail of your 2012–2022 window. Hung et al. 2024 work with 50 continuous GNSS stations, so a longer record exists somewhere.
No InSAR
Every competing group uses it — PS-InSAR, Sentinel-1, multi-temporal InSAR. Expect that as reviewer question #1. The leveling network is a defensible answer only if you make the argument explicitly.
Corroboration
The Sk-versus-coast correlation you found is real and published in spirit: Tsai & Hsu report the same proximal-to-distal gradient in elastic modulus. Cite it.
